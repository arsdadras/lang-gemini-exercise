# Teaching AI/LLM Application Engineering (agents, tools, graphs, RAG, evals, observability): approaches, evidence, and lesson patterns, 2024-2026

Researched 2026-09-28. Evidence labels used throughout:
**[EMP]** empirical study (peer-reviewed or arXiv with a stated method),
**[PRAC]** practitioner consensus or a named practitioner's writing,
**[VENDOR]** a framework or model vendor's guidance (has a commercial interest),
**[COURSE]** a course's published design (describes what is taught, not whether it works),
**[LS]** learning-science evidence from outside AI engineering (transfer to this subject is an inference).

## 1. Error analysis and "look at your data" as pedagogy (Husain and Shankar; EvalGen; criteria drift)

### Takeaway
The most-cited practitioner school (Husain, Shankar, and the applied-llms.org authors) teaches evals as a qualitative-research loop before any tooling: read ~30-100 real traces, write free-text failure notes (open coding), cluster them into a failure taxonomy (axial coding), count, then build binary pass/fail checks and align any LLM judge to human labels. The one peer-reviewed anchor for this is Shankar et al.'s "criteria drift" finding: people cannot fix grading criteria before they look at outputs, because grading is what defines the criteria.

### Cited Findings
- [PRAC] The evals FAQ teaches a fixed sequence: error analysis, then open coding (free-text notes on each failure), then axial coding (grouping notes into a failure taxonomy), then iterative refinement. It calls error analysis "the most important activity in evals." — [Husain & Shankar, "LLM Evals: Everything You Need to Know" / evals FAQ, hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) (the fetch reported a 2024-09-18 date, which conflicts with the page citing 5,000+ students; the page has probably been revised since, so treat the date as uncertain).
- [PRAC] Volumes they recommend: review at least ~30 traces by hand before bringing in any automation, and ~100 diverse traces in total, until "theoretical saturation," the point where new traces stop showing new failure modes. — [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- [PRAC] Binary pass/fail beats Likert (1-5) scales, because middle scores hide uncertainty. One domain expert (the "benevolent dictator") owns quality judgments, so the team avoids annotation-by-committee paralysis. — [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/); binary verdicts plus a written critique, [Husain, "A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products," 2025-03-24](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/)
- [PRAC] Synthetic data is built "dimensionally": name the axes of variation (in their recipe example: dietary restriction, cuisine, complexity), hand-write some tuples, then have an LLM (1) produce more tuples and (2) turn each tuple into a natural-language query. Don't just prompt "give me test queries." — [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/). The field guide names the dimensions as features, scenarios, and personas — [Field Guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/)
- [PRAC] Learner mistakes they list: generic off-the-shelf metrics (BERTScore, ROUGE, "helpfulness"); building evaluators before understanding the failures; outsourcing error analysis to people without domain expertise; tweaking prompts without measuring against human labels; handing an LLM judge the whole trace instead of scoped context. — [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- [PRAC] Teams should expect 60-80% of development time to go to error analysis and evaluation. — [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- [PRAC] Bottom-up error analysis loop: examine examples, write open notes, have an LLM help build the taxonomy, map examples to categories, count. Claim: three issues often account for 60%+ of problems. Build a custom data viewer (full context on one screen, one-key feedback, filtering); the guide says it gives much faster iteration than generic tools. Align automated judges to experts, aiming for >90% agreement before trusting them. Plan the roadmap by experiments run, not features shipped. — [Field Guide, 2025-03-24](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/)
- [PRAC] The earlier framework (March 2024) has three eval levels: L1 unit tests and assertions (cheap, run constantly), L2 human and model eval over logged traces with LLM critiques aligned to humans, L3 A/B tests. It claims failed AI products share one cause, no robust eval system. Case study: Rechat's "Lucy" assistant plateaued under prompt engineering until the team built failure-mode-driven tests. Key quotes: "Remove all friction from the process of looking at data," and you can never stop looking at data. — [Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals," 2024-03-29](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)
- [COURSE] "AI Evals For Engineers & PMs" (Maven): 4 weeks, 3-5 h/week, 15 live sessions across 10 lessons, 4 homework assignments with solution walkthroughs, a 200+ page course reader, and office hours. It claims 5,000+ students. The visible syllabus opens with "Building Agents You Can Evaluate," then "Building Agents, Synthetic Data & Scenarios." Other topics: instrumentation and logging, error analysis, LLM-as-judge and code-based evals, CI/CD wiring, red-teaming (prompt injection), and evaluating RAG, chatbots, multi-step agents, and multimodal systems. The page mentions a forthcoming O'Reilly book, *Evals for AI Engineers*. — [Maven course page](https://maven.com/parlance-labs/evals) (full syllabus not expanded on the page; the exact order of the later lessons was not verified)
- [PRAC] The six-author "year of building with LLMs" consensus: look at samples of LLM inputs and outputs every day; turn production samples into assertion-based unit tests; LLM-as-judge works best pairwise, with position-bias controls and chain of thought; don't over-index on one metric. — [Yan, Bischof, Frye, Husain, Liu, Shankar, "What We've Learned From A Year of Building with LLMs," 2024-06-08](https://applied-llms.org/) (2024; still widely cited, but it predates the agent-heavy practice of 2025-26)
- [EMP] "Who Validates the Validators?" (Shankar, Zamfirescu-Pereira, Hartmann, Parameswaran, Arawjo) presents EvalGen, a mixed-initiative tool. It proposes criteria and candidate assertions (Python functions or LLM grader prompts), asks the user to grade a subset of outputs, and keeps the implementations that agree with those grades. — [arXiv 2404.12272, 2024-04-18](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272); published at [UIST 2024 (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3654777.3676450)
- [EMP] Criteria drift: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria." Some criteria only appear after seeing specific outputs. The authors conclude eval assistants must support iterative criteria development rather than upfront specification. — [arXiv 2404.12272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272) (qualitative user study; this fetch did not return the participant count)
- [EMP] A 2025 multi-agent failure study built its taxonomy the same way: grounded analysis of 150 traces by expert annotators (inter-annotator kappa = 0.88), giving MAST, 14 failure modes in 3 categories (specification/system design, inter-agent misalignment, task verification), later extended to 1,600+ annotated traces across 7 frameworks. — [Cemri et al., "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?", arXiv 2503.13657](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.13657) (category percentages differ across secondary summaries and paper versions; check against the paper before quoting)

### Inferences
- This school's pedagogy is itself empirical method (qualitative coding from HCI and grounded theory), not a list of metrics. What a learner has to practice is reading, annotating, and counting failures, so the lesson form is a guided annotation session, not a lecture on metrics.
- Criteria drift justifies a mentor sequencing rule: never ask the learner to write eval criteria for a component before they have read its outputs. Look first, then define "good."
- MAST is a ready-made "reference taxonomy" to compare the learner's own open-coded taxonomy against, once they have built one (compare after, not before, or it anchors them).
- For a one-on-one chat format, the scaled-down version is realistic: 20-30 traces from one project (for example the P2 RAG graph or the P3 review pipeline), a free-text note per failure, grouping into 4-8 categories, a frequency count, then one binary check for the top category.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm the full ordered syllabus of the Husain and Shankar course, or the content of its four homework assignments, beyond the first two lessons.
- No controlled study found that measures whether learners taught error-analysis-first build better systems than learners taught metrics-first. The support is practitioner consensus plus the criteria-drift HCI finding.
- The participant count in the EvalGen study was not captured in this pass.

## 2. Whole-game / top-down (fast.ai, Perkins), build-from-scratch (Karpathy), and "build it then break it": what supports each

### Takeaway
Each approach has a strong practitioner following, and there are no controlled studies comparing them for AI engineers. The adjacent learning-science evidence points three ways: problem-first sequencing helps older learners, active prediction beats passive demos, and heavy worked-example guidance loses value, or backfires, as expertise grows. Together these favor a whole-game frame with failure-first, prediction-driven episodes and faded scaffolding for an advanced learner. "Build from scratch" is best read, for application engineering, as "implement the pattern on raw APIs before (or next to) the framework," the stance Anthropic and Andrew Ng both take.

### Cited Findings
- [PRAC] fast.ai's Jeremy Howard teaches top-down: from lesson one, learners train real models on real data. He cites David Perkins' *Making Learning Whole* ("whole game") as an inspiration, with the soccer analogy: play the game before studying ball manufacture and physics. — [Medium summary, "Fast.AI, David Perkins & Pedagogy"](https://medium.com/@minhaaj/fast-ai-david-perkins-pedagogy-1f0155639e76); [course.fast.ai](https://course.fast.ai/) (these are secondary summaries; Perkins' book dates from 2009 and is a theory of instruction, not an RCT)
- [PRAC] Karpathy's "Neural Networks: Zero to Hero" builds bottom-up from scratch: micrograd (backprop), makemore (bigram, MLP, BatchNorm, manual backprop, WaveNet), GPT from scratch, then the GPT tokenizer. Prerequisites are solid Python and intro math. Rationale: language models are "an excellent place to learn deep learning." — [karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero](https://karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero.html) (undated and marked "ongoing"; it covers model internals, not application engineering)
- [PRAC] At the application layer the analogue is raw-API-first. Anthropic advises starting with LLM APIs directly ("many patterns can be implemented in a few lines of code"), and warns that frameworks hide the underlying prompts and responses, which makes debugging harder. — [Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024-12-19](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- [COURSE] Andrew Ng's "Agentic AI" course is vendor-neutral, in raw Python, building each pattern "from first principles before exploring frameworks." — [DeepLearning.AI course page](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/agentic-ai/); [Batch announcement](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/check-out-our-course-on-how-to-build-ai-agents)
- [LS][EMP] Productive failure. A meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons) found problem-solving-then-instruction beats instruction-then-problem-solving for conceptual understanding and transfer, Hedges' g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51], rising to 0.37-0.58 with high fidelity to productive-failure design. Older learners (grade 6 through university) benefited more. — [Sinha & Kapur, Review of Educational Research, 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105) (domains: math and science, not software)
- [LS][EMP] Passive demonstrations: students who only watch understand no better than students who never saw the demo. Students who predict the outcome first show significantly better understanding, at little time cost. — [Crouch, Fagen, Callan, Mazur, Am. J. Physics 72, 2004](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment) (older physics study; the mechanism transfers plausibly to live agent demos)
- [LS][EMP] Expertise reversal effect: methods that help novices (detailed worked examples, redundant explanations) lose their benefit or hurt more experienced learners, who do better when guidance fades into completion problems. — [Kalyuga, introduction to the Instructional Science special issue, 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-009-9102-0); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)
- [LS] PRIMM (Predict-Run-Investigate-Modify-Make), introduced by Sue Sentance in 2017, structures programming lessons around predicting what given code does before running it, then investigating and modifying it, before writing new code. Summaries report a 2019 multi-school evaluation with better comprehension than comparison groups. — [ACM WiPSCE 2017](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3137065.3137084); [PRIMM project page](https://computingeducationresearch.org/projects/primm/) (K-12 context; this pass did not verify the 2019 effect sizes)

### Inferences
- For an advanced adult learner who reviews verified code instead of typing it, the best-supported combination is: whole-game framing per project (run the real system first), PRIMM-style predict-run-investigate on the real code, productive-failure episodes (show or induce a failure, let the learner diagnose it before seeing the fix), and faded explanation (expertise reversal: skip what they already know).
- "Build from scratch" maps onto this curriculum as "show the raw call under the abstraction." For example: have the learner reconstruct from a trace the exact prompt and tool schema that a LangChain or DeepAgents call sent. That keeps Karpathy's demystification benefit without typing.
- "Build it then break it" has no direct AI-engineering evidence. It is best justified via productive failure and prediction: breaking a component (removing a tool description, a guardrail, a reducer) is a controlled failure the learner must predict and then explain.

### Gaps
- No controlled or quasi-experimental study was found comparing top-down, bottom-up, or break-it teaching specifically for LLM application engineering.
- The empirical base behind the fast.ai method (beyond popularity and testimonials) was not found. Perkins' framework is theoretical.
- I did not locate the original "Build It, Break It, Fix It" (security-contest pedagogy) paper in this pass, so it is not cited.

## 3. How framework vendors and major courses teach agents, and the criticism of tutorial or framework-tour teaching

### Takeaway
Vendor courses (LangChain Academy, Hugging Face) are organized around framework features: graphs, state, memory, HITL, deployment. Model labs and independent teachers (Anthropic, OpenAI's guide, Ng, Huyen) are organized around patterns and decisions: workflow vs agent, tool design, evals first, single agent before multi-agent. The recurring critique of framework-first teaching, and of frameworks themselves, is that abstractions hide the prompt and harden before usage patterns exist. That critique comes from practitioners, not from education research.

### Cited Findings
- [COURSE][VENDOR] LangChain Academy "Introduction to LangGraph": 6 modules, 55 lessons, ~6 h of video, free. Module 1 covers simple graphs, LangSmith Studio, chains, routers, agents, and agent memory. Module 2: state schemas, reducers, multiple schemas, message trimming, summarization, external memory. Module 3: streaming, breakpoints, state editing, dynamic breakpoints, time travel. Module 4: parallelization, sub-graphs, map-reduce, a research assistant. Module 5: long-term memory (Store, profiles, collections). Module 6: deployment, double-texting, assistants. — [LangChain Academy course page](https://academy.langchain.com/courses/intro-to-langgraph) (no date on the page; possibly predates LangGraph 1.0 APIs, not verified)
- [COURSE][VENDOR] LangChain Academy also offers a Deep Agents course: building long-running agents with the Deep Agents harness, what a harness is, and using LangSmith to improve agents, with notebooks at [langchain-ai/lca-deepagents](https://github.com/langchain-ai/lca-deepagents). It also offers a "Deep Research with LangGraph" project course (multi-agent research plus LangSmith evaluation). — [Deep Agents course page](https://academy.langchain.com/courses/foundation-introduction-to-deepagents); [Deep Research project](https://academy.langchain.com/courses/deep-research-with-langgraph) (module list not verified)
- [COURSE] Hugging Face Agents Course: Unit 0 onboarding; Unit 1, the Thought-Action-Observation loop, tools, messages, and chat templates; Unit 2, the same ideas implemented in three frameworks (smolagents, LlamaIndex, LangGraph); Unit 3, agentic RAG; Unit 4, a final agent certified against a benchmark. Bonus units cover function-calling fine-tuning, agent observability and evaluation, and agents in games. Free certificates. — [huggingface/agents-course (GitHub)](https://github.com/huggingface/agents-course); [unit 0 intro](https://huggingface.co/learn/agents-course/en/unit0/introduction); the unit summary is via [ExpertBeacon review](https://expertbeacon.com/hugging-face-ai-agents-course-in-2026-what-you-learn-and-whether-its-worth-your-time/) (secondary)
- [COURSE] DeepLearning.AI "Agentic AI" (Andrew Ng; announced ~October 2025 per his LinkedIn post): ~10 h, 31 videos, 7 code examples, 8 graded assignments. Modules: (1) Intro to agentic workflows, (2) Reflection, (3) Tool use, (4) Practical tips for building agentic AI (evals and error analysis), (5) Patterns for highly autonomous agents (planning, multi-agent). Projects: research agent, chart generation, SQL generation with reflection, email assistant, customer-service agent, multi-agent market-research team. Covers end-to-end vs component-level evals and "error analysis and prioritizing next steps." Its framing: the biggest difference between effective and less effective agent builders is a disciplined process of evals and error analysis. — [course page](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/agentic-ai/); [LinkedIn announcement](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/andrewyng_announcing-my-new-course-agentic-ai-building-activity-7381380126317404160-wW75); [eval lesson](https://learn.deeplearning.ai/courses/agentic-ai/lesson/46yh5j/evaluating-agentic-ai-(evals))
- [VENDOR] Anthropic, "Building effective agents": workflows ("predefined code paths") vs agents (LLMs "dynamically direct their own processes"). Five workflow patterns: prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, orchestrator-workers, evaluator-optimizer. Use agents only for open-ended problems with unpredictable step counts. Three principles: simplicity, transparency (show planning steps), and a carefully engineered agent-computer interface (ACI). Poka-yoke tool design example: requiring absolute file paths eliminated the model's relative-path mistakes. — [anthropic.com/engineering, 2024-12-19](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- [VENDOR] Anthropic, "Writing effective tools for agents": a four-step loop (prototype, run an evaluation with realistic multi-tool tasks, analyze transcripts and metrics, improve, including with Claude Code's help), and a held-out test set to avoid overfitting. Principles: target high-impact workflows instead of wrapping every endpoint ("more tools don't always lead to better outcomes"), namespacing, high-signal returns, token efficiency (pagination, filtering, truncation), and careful tool descriptions, where "even small refinements ... can yield dramatic improvements." — [anthropic.com/engineering, 2025-09-11](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)
- [VENDOR] OpenAI, "A practical guide to building agents" (free PDF, ~34 pages): maximize a single agent's capabilities before going multi-agent. Guardrails are layered defense combining LLM-based checks, rules-based checks (regex), and moderation, with human-in-the-loop intervention for high-risk actions. — [OpenAI guide page](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/); [PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf) (2025; details via search summary)
- [PRAC] Chip Huyen, *AI Engineering* (O'Reilly, 2025): chapters on foundation models, evaluation methodology (ch. 3), evaluating AI systems (ch. 4, building an evaluation pipeline, AI-as-judge), prompt engineering, RAG and agents, finetuning, dataset engineering, and inference optimization. Evaluation gets two chapters before RAG and agents. — [chiphuyen/aie-book chapter summaries](https://github.com/chiphuyen/aie-book/blob/main/chapter-summaries.md); [repo](https://github.com/chiphuyen/aie-book)
- [PRAC] Critique of framework abstractions: Husain's "Fuck You, Show Me The Prompt" argues LLM frameworks that write prompts for you are an opaque abstraction. Code should handle routing, retries, and chaining; prompts should stay visible natural language. — [hamel.dev/blog/posts/prompt](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/prompt/) (early 2024, exact date not verified)
- [PRAC] Octomind used LangChain in production for ~12 months (2023-2024), then removed it. Their stated reasons: its abstractions hid the lower-level code they needed, they kept having to dig into its internals, and the abstractions were defined before the usage patterns existed. — [Octomind blog](https://octomind.dev/blog/why-we-no-longer-use-langchain-for-building-our-ai-agents/index.html); [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40739982) (mid-2024; predates LangChain/LangGraph 1.0, so parts may be superseded)
- [VENDOR] LangChain's rebuttal frame: LangGraph is a low-level orchestration framework with agent abstractions on top. Apps sit on a workflow-to-agent spectrum, and the hard part of reliable agents is giving the LLM the right context. Workflows help because they decide exactly how data flows. — [Harrison Chase, "How to think about agent frameworks" (~April 2025)](https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-to-think-about-agent-frameworks) (written partly as a response to OpenAI's guide)

### Inferences
- Vendor courses teach "what the framework offers" in feature order (reducers, breakpoints, Store). The model-lab and independent sources teach "what decision you are making" (workflow or agent, which tool surface, how you will know it works). A production-track mentor should use the second as the spine and the first as reference material. This matches the curriculum's existing rule against touring framework docs.
- The Hugging Face course's "same ideas in three frameworks" design is a deliberate counter to framework lock-in. For a single-stack curriculum (LangChain, LangGraph, DeepAgents), the same benefit comes cheaper from occasionally showing the raw provider call or the rendered prompt behind a framework call.
- Evals appear early or centrally in the pattern-first sources (Ng's module 4 before autonomous patterns; Huyen's chapters 3-4 before RAG and agents; the Husain and Shankar course opening with "agents you can evaluate"). That suggests evals belong in every project, not as a late standalone unit.

### Gaps
- Found no systematic or published critique of LangChain Academy or the Hugging Face course specifically (reviews exist, but they are blog-level). The "framework-tour" critique is aimed at frameworks and tutorial culture in general.
- Did not verify the Deep Agents course's module list, or whether Intro to LangGraph was updated for LangGraph 1.x.
- Anthropic's own course catalog (Anthropic Academy / "Claude courses") was not examined in this pass.

## 4. University courses on LLM systems and agents, 2024-2026: pedagogy, projects, assessment

### Takeaway
University offerings split into (a) lecture-series and seminar courses on agent research (Berkeley's LLM Agents MOOCs, Stanford CS329A), assessed by labs, hackathons, and research projects, and (b) project and studio courses on building LLM apps (Stanford CS224G), assessed by team products, sprints, and demo days. Neither kind publishes evidence of learning outcomes. Production-engineering rigor (evals, observability, failure handling) is a listed topic in the app-building courses, but the pedagogy is startup-style iteration, not deliberate practice of error analysis.

### Cited Findings
- [COURSE] Berkeley CS294/194-196 "Large Language Model Agents" MOOC (Fall 2024): guest-lecture series. MOOC certificate tiers required passing 3 lab assignments. Higher tiers required an article on a hackathon submission, graded pass/no-pass. The companion LLM Agents Hackathon drew ~3,000 participants across 5 tracks (Applications, Benchmarks, Fundamentals, Safety, Decentralized & Multi-Agents). — [MOOC F24 page](https://llmagents-learning.org/f24); [f24 syllabus on GitHub](https://github.com/rdi-berkeley/llm-agents-mooc/blob/main/f24.md); [Hackathon](https://rdi.berkeley.edu/llm-agents-hackathon). Followed by CS294/194-280 "Advanced LLM Agents" (Spring 2025), [intro slides](https://rdi.berkeley.edu/adv-llm-agents/slides/llm-agents-berkeley-intro-sp25.pdf). An "Agentic AI" offering with 2025 slides also exists at [rdi.berkeley.edu/agentic-ai](https://rdi.berkeley.edu/agentic-ai/slides/introduction_25.pdf) (content not reviewed).
- [COURSE] Stanford CS329A "Self-Improving AI Agents" (instructors Aakanksha Chowdhery and Azalia Mirhoseini): covers self-improvement (constitutional AI, verifiers, test-time compute scaling, search plus LLMs, RL), tool use, code, memory, and multimodal orchestration. Grading is 50% homework, 50% project. Projects run in teams of 2-4 with API credits and must be a new eval dataset or benchmark, an agentic-system reliability study, or hill-climbing an existing benchmark. - [COURSE] Stanford CS224G "Building and Scaling LLM Applications" (Winter 2025, Winter 2026): project-based, teams of 2-5, two-week sprints ending in a Demo Day, startup-style iteration. Topics include reasoning models, agentic architectures, RAG and context engineering, prompt optimization, LLM evaluation and testing, multi-agent systems, production deployment, observability and monitoring, and cost/latency trade-offs. — [CS224G home](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224g/); [schedule](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224g/schedule.html); [projects](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224g/projects.html); [outcomes](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224g/outcomes.html)
- [COURSE] CMU 17-645 "Machine Learning in Production" (cross-listed 11-695 "AI Engineering"; Christian Kästner) covers building, deploying, assuring, and maintaining ML-enabled products: lifecycle, responsible AI (safety, security, fairness, explainability), MLOps. It has public slides and assignments and a textbook (MIT Press, April 2025). CMU also lists 17-630 Prompt Engineering. — [course site](https://mlip-cmu.github.io/); [book](https://mlip-cmu.github.io/book/); [Kästner teaching page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ckaestne/teaching.html) (this pass did not confirm the extent of LLM or agent content)
- [EMP] Computing-education research (SIGCSE 2025-2026) on generative AI is dominated by how students *use* GenAI tools (for example in database courses, and LLM-generated-code detection). Searches found no SIGCSE or ITiCSE paper evaluating a course that teaches students to *build* LLM applications. — [SIGCSE TS 2025 example paper](https://sigcse2025.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2025-Papers/3/Understanding-the-Impact-of-Using-Generative-AI-Tools-in-a-Database-Course); [SIGCSE TS 2026 example](https://sigcse2026.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2026-Papers/44/LLM-Based-Explainable-Detection-of-LLM-Generated-Code-in-Python-Programming-Courses). A 2026 arXiv experience report on building LLM multi-agent systems in software engineering exists but was not reviewed. — [arXiv 2608.11965](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11965v1)

### Inferences
- CS329A's project rule (benchmark, reliability study, or hill-climbing, never "just an app") is the strongest university signal that *measurement* is the assessable skill in agent engineering. It maps onto the curriculum as: each project's capstone includes a small measured claim (for example "the grader passes 18/20 before and 20/20 after, over 5 trials").
- Studio courses (CS224G) prize demos and iteration speed. The production-track curriculum can borrow the demo-day ritual (live run plus teach-back) while holding a stricter bar: every demo shows at least one failure case and its trace.

### Gaps
- No outcome data (learning gains, retention) was found for any of these courses.
- Did not verify CMU 11-667 or any CMU course dedicated to LLM agents. Did not check MIT, Princeton, or other 2025-26 agent courses.
- Assessment rubrics for CS224G were not captured.

## 5. Teaching non-determinism and probabilistic behavior: repeated runs, variance, traces, ablations, failure-first demos, model comparison

### Takeaway
What makes this subject different from classic programming is well documented. Outputs vary even at temperature 0. Reliability must be measured over repeated trials (pass^k), not a single run. Correctness is judged by graders (code, model, human) over transcripts and outcomes, not only by unit tests. The system's decision logic lives in prompts and traces, not only in code. Established teaching moves follow from this: run a scenario k times and show the spread, read transcripts before trusting a score, separate capability evals from regression evals, and ablate components against a small eval set.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Temperature 0 is not deterministic in practice. Sampling 1,000 completions at temperature 0 from Qwen3-235B produced 80 distinct outputs, first diverging at token 103. The root cause is a lack of batch invariance in inference kernels: a request's numerics depend on concurrent load. Batch-invariant kernels restored bitwise-identical outputs. — [Thinking Machines Lab, "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference," Sept 2025](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)
- [EMP] τ-bench introduced pass^k, the probability that all k independent trials succeed. It found large consistency gaps: a GPT-4o agent at ~61% single-trial success in the retail domain fell to ~25% when all 8 trials had to succeed. — [Yao et al., τ-bench, arXiv 2406.12045, June 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045); [Sierra blog](https://sierra.ai/blog/tau-bench-shaping-development-evaluation-agents) (figures via search summary; 2024 models, so absolute numbers are outdated but the gap phenomenon is the lesson)
- [VENDOR] Anthropic's agent-eval vocabulary: *task* (inputs plus success criteria), *trial* (one attempt; run several because outputs vary), *grader* (code-based, model-based, human), *transcript* (full record of outputs, tool calls, reasoning), *outcome* (final environment state, not what the agent claims). pass@k rises with k while pass^k falls: "By k=10, they tell opposite stories." Start with 20-50 tasks drawn from real failures, because early changes have large effects. Reading transcripts is "non-negotiable," since a failure may be the agent's or a grader rejecting a valid solution. Capability evals (low pass rate, hill-climb) "graduate" into regression evals (~100%). A good task is one where "two domain experts would independently reach the same pass/fail verdict." — [Anthropic, "Demystifying evals for AI agents," 2026-01-09](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- [VENDOR][PRAC] Traces as the new source of truth: when decision logic moves into the model, the source of truth moves from code to traces. Debugging becomes sharing a trace and commenting on specific decision points, and observability becomes a collaboration tool. — [LangChain blog, "In software, the code documents the app. In AI, the traces do." (Jan 2026)](https://blog.langchain.com/in-software-the-code-documents-the-app-in-ai-the-traces-do/)
- [EMP] Prompts behave like programs, but developers struggle to form mental models of them. An interview study (20 developers, grounded theory) found prompt programmers build mental models of *the foundation model's behavior on the prompt* by interacting with it, and even experienced developers struggle to form reliable ones. The authors conclude prompt programming differs from traditional software development. — [Liang, Lin, Rao, Myers, "Prompts Are Programs Too!", FSE 2025, arXiv 2409.12447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12447)
- [EMP] Professional copilot builders (26 engineers interviewed) called prompt engineering "more of an art than a science," described models as "very, very fragile," and reported testing as extremely time-consuming, with pain at every stage of the engineering process. — [Parnin et al., "Building Your Own Product Copilot," arXiv 2312.14231, SANER 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14231)
- [PRAC] Karpathy's "Software 3.0" (YC AI Startup School, June 2025): natural-language prompts are programs that run on LLMs. Successful products use partial autonomy with an "autonomy slider," keeping a human in the loop instead of aiming for full autonomy at once. — [Latent Space write-up](https://www.latent.space/p/s3)
- [PRAC] Pin model versions in production to avoid unexpected behavior changes. Build evals and monitoring that enable rapid iteration. — [applied-llms.org, 2024-06-08](https://applied-llms.org/)
- [COURSE] Observability as curriculum content: the Hugging Face Agents Course has a bonus unit on agent observability and evaluation. — [huggingface/agents-course](https://github.com/huggingface/agents-course). LangChain Academy puts LangSmith Studio in Module 1 and time-travel debugging in Module 3. — [Intro to LangGraph](https://academy.langchain.com/courses/intro-to-langgraph)
- [VENDOR] Ablation and iteration against held-out tasks is Anthropic's recommended tool-improvement loop (prototype, evaluate, analyze transcripts, improve, check a holdout set). — [Writing effective tools for agents, 2025-09-11](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)
- [LS][EMP] Failure-first sequencing is supported by productive-failure evidence (g = 0.36 in a 53-study meta-analysis) — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105). Prediction before a demo improves learning from it — [Crouch et al. 2004](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment)

### Inferences
- **Why this subject differs from classic programming (a teaching summary):** (1) the same input gives a distribution of outputs, so "it worked when I ran it" is a sample of one; (2) correctness is a rate measured by graders, so tests become evals with thresholds; (3) behavior is partly specified in natural language (prompts, tool descriptions), which is code without a compiler or type checker; (4) the execution path is chosen at runtime by the model, so the trace, not the source, documents what happened; (5) the evaluator (LLM judge) is itself probabilistic and must be validated (criteria drift, judge-human agreement).
- Concrete patterns this supports, for a one-on-one chat lesson on a verified codebase:
  - **Variance run:** the learner predicts how many of 5 runs of one scenario (test mode) will pass, then runs them, tabulates, and names pass@5 vs pass^5. Repeat after a change to see whether variance, not just the mean, moved.
  - **Trace walk:** open one LangSmith trace and have the learner narrate each decision point ("why did the router pick this branch; what context did it see?") before the mentor comments. This is the traces-as-documentation idea turned into a reading exercise.
  - **Show-me-the-prompt:** from the trace, have the learner reconstruct the actual system prompt and tool schemas a framework call sent, and name what the framework added.
  - **Ablation "what if we remove X":** remove one component (a reranker, a tool description sentence, a guardrail, the reflection step, the HITL gate). The learner predicts the effect, runs a 10-20 task mini-eval, and compares. The prediction is recorded before the run.
  - **Failure-first demo:** show the failing run (and its trace) before any fix; the learner diagnoses and proposes a fix; then reveal the engineer's verified fix and compare.
  - **Model comparison:** run the same small eval set across two tiers or providers and build a quality/latency/cost table. The lesson is that model choice is an empirical decision per component.
  - **Grader-vs-agent attribution:** give a failing transcript and ask "is this the agent's failure or the grader's?", per Anthropic's non-negotiable transcript reading.
  - **Capability-to-regression graduation:** after a fix, the learner decides which eval tasks move into the regression suite and why.

### Gaps
- No education study was found measuring whether repeated-run or variance demonstrations improve learners' calibration about LLM reliability. The support is practitioner guidance plus general prediction and productive-failure research.
- The claim that LLM-app teaching specifically benefits from ablations is an inference from Anthropic's and the evals school's workflows, not from a teaching study.

## 6. Teaching design judgment: workflow vs agent, deterministic code vs LLM decisions, guardrails, failure contracts

### Takeaway
Practitioners teach design judgment as a default toward the least autonomy that works, raised only when evidence demands it. The heuristics: workflows before agents (Anthropic), a single agent before multi-agent (OpenAI), deterministic plans and structured outputs over free autonomy (applied-llms.org), a workflow-to-agent spectrum where predictability is the price of agency (LangChain), and an autonomy slider with a human in the loop (Karpathy). Guardrails are taught as layered defense, not a single filter. This is taught through named patterns and case contrasts, not formal method; "failure contracts" as a teaching topic barely appears in these sources.

### Cited Findings
- [VENDOR] Workflows (predefined code paths) vs agents (the model directs its own process). Prefer the simplest solution; optimizing a single call with retrieval often suffices. Agents only for open-ended problems where step counts can't be predicted. They cost more and demand more testing. — [Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024-12-19](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
- [VENDOR] Maximize one agent before adding more. Multi-agent separation adds complexity and overhead. — [OpenAI, "A practical guide to building agents" (2025)](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/)
- [VENDOR] Guardrails as layered defense: no single guardrail suffices. Combine LLM-based checks, rules (regex), moderation, tool-risk ratings, and human-in-the-loop intervention. — [OpenAI guide PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf) (via search summary)
- [VENDOR] The spectrum view: the more agentic, the less predictable. Predictability matters for trust and regulation. Real systems are often hybrids. The core difficulty is getting the right context to the model. — [Chase, "How to think about agent frameworks" (~April 2025)](https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-to-think-about-agent-frameworks)
- [PRAC] Prefer deterministic execution plans to unconstrained agent autonomy for reliability. Decompose tasks into focused steps. Use structured outputs for downstream integration. Put guardrails on hallucination and unwanted content. Keep humans in the loop through HITL UX. — [applied-llms.org, 2024-06-08](https://applied-llms.org/)
- [PRAC] Partial autonomy and the autonomy slider: hand tasks to AI gradually with human verification. — [Karpathy, Software 3.0, June 2025 (Latent Space)](https://www.latent.space/p/s3)
- [PRAC] Husain's split: code handles routing, function execution, retries, and chaining; natural language handles prompt construction. This is a principled boundary between deterministic code and LLM work. — [hamel.dev "Show Me The Prompt"](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/prompt/)
- [VENDOR] Tool design as interface design (ACI, poka-yoke): invest in tools as much as in human UIs, and change arguments so mistakes become impossible (absolute paths). — [Anthropic, Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); fewer, higher-level tools and namespacing — [Anthropic, Writing effective tools](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)
- [EMP] In multi-agent systems, most failures trace to specification and system design and to inter-agent misalignment, not only model capability; task-verification failures are the third category. This supports teaching "design before model upgrades." — [Cemri et al., MAST, arXiv 2503.13657](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)
- [COURSE] Ng's course sequences patterns from least to most autonomous (reflection, tool use, evals and practical tips, then planning and multi-agent), a curriculum-level embodiment of "earn autonomy." — [DeepLearning.AI Agentic AI](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/agentic-ai/)

### Inferences
- A usable **design-judgment kata** for chat lessons: give the learner a feature from the next project and ask them to (1) place it on the workflow-to-agent spectrum, (2) name every decision point and say whether plain code, a structured LLM call, or an agent loop should own it, (3) name the guardrail layers and the HITL gate, and (4) state the failure contract (what the component returns when a dependency fails). Then compare the answer with the engineer's verified design and with the Anthropic, OpenAI, and LangChain heuristics. Disagreements are discussion material, not errors.
- **Case contrast:** show two designs of the same component (for example a router as an LLM call vs as rules) with their eval results and traces, and ask which one the learner would ship at production load and why. The admissible arguments are measured quality, failure behavior, and predictability, not "it's only a CLI."
- The sources are thin on failure contracts (typed failure envelopes, retry layering, timeouts). The repo's own conventions (one retry layer per call, `failed` envelopes at specialist boundaries) are more specific than anything found, so the mentor should teach them from the repo, citing general reliability principles.

### Gaps
- No source found that teaches "failure contracts" for LLM components as an explicit curriculum topic. It appears only implicitly (guardrails, HITL, structured outputs).
- No empirical study was found on how design judgment for agentic systems is best taught (case method vs pattern catalogs).

## 7. Common misconceptions and failure modes of learners building LLM apps

### Takeaway
Documented misconceptions cluster in five areas: exploring prompts opportunistically instead of systematically and overgeneralizing from a few examples; trusting outputs and vibe-checks over measured evals; tweaking prompts without a baseline; reaching for generic metrics or tools instead of domain-specific error analysis; and confusing framework APIs with the underlying concepts. The strongest empirical sources are HCI studies of prompt designers and interview studies of professional builders. The rest is practitioner consensus.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Non-AI-experts designing chatbot prompts explored prompt designs "opportunistically, not systematically," and struggled in ways that echo known end-user-programming and interactive-ML difficulties. — [Zamfirescu-Pereira, Wong, Hartmann, Yang, "Why Johnny Can't Prompt," CHI 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581388) (2023, older, but it is the foundational study; its participants were non-experts, so transfer to an advanced engineer is partial)
- [EMP] Even experienced prompt programmers struggle to form reliable mental models of model behavior. — [Liang et al., FSE 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12447)
- [EMP] Professional builders call prompt engineering "more of an art than a science" and find testing extremely time-consuming, with pain at every step. — [Parnin et al., SANER 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14231)
- [EMP] Criteria drift: evaluators' criteria change as they see outputs. Assuming fixed upfront criteria is itself a misconception. — [Shankar et al., UIST 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)
- [PRAC] Common mistakes: generic metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore, "helpfulness"), building evaluators before looking at failures, outsourcing error analysis, prompt tweaking without human-label measurement, Likert scales, full-trace judge inputs. — [Husain & Shankar evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- [PRAC] The "tools trap": teams fixate on frameworks and dashboards instead of measurement and iteration. — [Husain, Field Guide, 2025-03-24](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/)
- [PRAC] Products plateau under prompt engineering without an eval system (Rechat case). — [Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals," 2024-03-29](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)
- [VENDOR] Mistaking framework abstraction for understanding: frameworks "obscure the underlying prompts and responses," and incorrect assumptions about what is under the hood are a common source of error. — [Anthropic, Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); practitioner echo, [Octomind](https://octomind.dev/blog/why-we-no-longer-use-langchain-for-building-our-ai-agents/index.html)
- [VENDOR] Judging an agent by what it says instead of the final environment state: Anthropic separates *outcome* (actual state) from the transcript's claims. — [Demystifying evals for AI agents, 2026-01-09](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- [EMP] Temperature 0 as a guarantee of determinism is false in practice. — [Thinking Machines, Sept 2025](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/). A single success is not reliability (pass^k gap). — [τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)
- [EMP] Multi-agent failures are mostly specification and coordination problems (MAST), countering the misconception that "a better model will fix it." — [Cemri et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)
- [VENDOR] "More tools" is not better; tool descriptions are a high-leverage prompt. — [Anthropic, Writing effective tools](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)

### Inferences

#### Misconception-to-lesson map (for the mentor)
| Misconception | Diagnostic question to ask the learner | Corrective lesson pattern |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "It worked once, so it works" | "How many of 5 runs will pass? What is pass^5?" | Variance run (Q5) |
| "Temperature 0 means deterministic" | "Why might two identical calls differ?" | Show two diverging traces; cite batch invariance |
| "The agent did X because it said so" | "What is the outcome state, not the claim?" | Outcome-vs-transcript check on a real run |
| "A better prompt will fix it" (unmeasured tweaking) | "What is the baseline pass rate and on which tasks?" | Before/after on a fixed mini-eval, prediction recorded first |
| "Use a standard metric" | "Which failure in our traces does this metric catch?" | Open-coding session (Q1), then one binary check |
| "The LLM judge is ground truth" | "How often does the judge agree with you on 20 labels?" | Judge-alignment exercise; criteria-drift reflection |
| "LangGraph/DeepAgents feature = concept" | "What prompt and what state update did this call produce?" | Show-me-the-prompt from the trace |
| "More agents or tools = more capable" | "Which MAST category would this failure fall into?" | Design kata (Q6); tool-surface ablation |
| "Criteria first, then look" | "Write the criteria only after reading 20 outputs" | Sequencing rule from criteria drift |

#### Consolidated lesson-pattern catalog (synthesized from the sources above; practitioner-plus-learning-science inference, not a tested curriculum)
1. **Predict-Run-Investigate on verified code** (PRIMM plus Crouch): before each live demo the learner writes a prediction (output, path, trace shape, pass count). Run, compare, explain the gap.
2. **Failure-first episode** (productive failure): the failing run and trace come first; the learner diagnoses; then reveal the engineer's fix.
3. **Trace walk and show-me-the-prompt:** traces as the primary documentation of runtime behavior.
4. **Mini error analysis:** 20-30 traces, open notes, axial categories, counts, one binary eval for the top category. Compare with MAST afterwards.
5. **Judge alignment:** the learner labels ~20 outputs; compute agreement with the LLM judge; discuss disagreements and how the criteria drifted.
6. **Variance and pass^k run:** the same scenario k times; distinguish capability evals from regression evals.
7. **Ablation "what if we remove X"** on a fixed mini-eval, with the prediction recorded first.
8. **Model or tier comparison:** quality, latency, and cost per component on the same tasks.
9. **Design kata:** spectrum placement, decision-point ownership, guardrail layers, HITL gate, failure contract; compare with the verified design.
10. **Teach-back framed as defense:** the learner explains *why* the design is what it is and what failure each part prevents (aligned with the curriculum's success criterion). Scaffolding fades as competence grows (expertise reversal).

### Gaps
- No study was found cataloguing misconceptions of *advanced software engineers* (as opposed to non-experts) learning agent engineering specifically.
- No empirical evaluation was found of teach-back or protégé-effect methods in AI-engineering education (not searched in depth in this pass).
- Security misconceptions (prompt injection, secret exposure in sandboxes) are mentioned as course topics (Husain and Shankar red-teaming; OpenAI guardrails), but no teaching-focused source on them was reviewed.
